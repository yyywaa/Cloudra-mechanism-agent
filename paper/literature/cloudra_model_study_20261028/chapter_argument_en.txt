Cloudra addresses two connected transitions in autonomous mechanism research: from chemical intent to a concrete spatial operation, and from local computational results to an evidence-supported scientific claim. Both require the model's decisions to remain attached to specific chemical objects. During structure preparation, these objects are atoms, fragments, and geometric relationships. During scientific delivery, they are species, elementary steps, and the calculations supporting them. This correspondence connects the system's methods throughout an extended research process.

The experimental scope is introduced through both chemical coverage and the research questions posed by the tasks. Reaction classes, changes in bonding, and stereochemical features describe the chemical distribution. Task semantics describe what the agent must decide: how to prepare a configuration, compare competing pathways, identify a stereochemical outcome, or assess accessibility. Chemically similar reactions can pose different research questions, while different reactions can require related operations. These complementary views explain how the benchmark exercises spatial manipulation and scientific delivery.

The first transition begins with chemical identity and proceeds through geometric measurement and execution. Local comparisons showed that judgments from XYZ coordinates varied with reasoning mode and available budget, whereas programmatically measured distances made the relevant comparison more direct. These observations suggest a practical role for geometric tools: they carry out recurring numerical work while the model selects goals and compares candidates. Chemical identity, atom mapping, and reaction-center definitions preserve the target across representations. Internal-coordinate measurements, fragment operations, reactant preorganization, and endpoint alignment then translate that target into configurations for subsequent calculations.

The E1 trajectory illustrates how these components work together. The agent intended to bring the H7–C2–C3–Cl4 dihedral close to 180°, but the tool's representative dihedral involved H5. The agent performed an additional measurement of its actual H7 target. This gave approximately 59.4° for candidate A and 179.4° for candidate B, after which it selected B for the next stage. The sequence connects an intended chemical change, a local transformation, a targeted observation, and a subsequent choice. It also makes the purpose of feedback concrete: the returned measurement must answer the question currently guiding the research.

A local comparison based on this trajectory connected the observations to subsequent actions. With only the H5 measurements available, the model requested measurement of H7. Once the target H7 values were supplied, it selected candidate B. This change was preserved across three rotations of the response options. The target-specific observation thus provided a direct basis for the next choice.

The second transition concerns the interpretation of accumulated calculations. The delivery DAG organizes structures, energies, frequencies, IRC trajectories, and endpoint results around species and elementary steps. Local association comparisons showed that explicit links helped the model check which results belonged together; additional reasoning also corrected earlier judgments about these relationships. This suggests that the evidence graph can reduce the repeated work of locating and assembling supporting records. Separately recording execution, numerical convergence, and scientific support clarifies what each result contributes to the current claim.

The DAG also preserves a queryable research state: what evidence each object already has, what a new result changes, and what remains to be done. It provides an explicit starting point for the next reasoning cycle. Short reports and a window containing twelve original messages showed that the local model could identify outstanding requirements when these were presented. Internal coordinates expose a geometric state that can be manipulated; the DAG exposes a scientific state that can be advanced. Together they connect language-based decisions to persistent research objects.

Three formal trajectories show how scientific feedback enters subsequent actions. In one, truncating an entire result hid the blocking object; a targeted read exposed it, followed by a concrete reaction-center modification. In another, detailed IRC endpoint-association diagnostics were removed, and the agent changed a source-minimum reference while the same linkage gap remained. In a third, a zero-imaginary-frequency result was followed by a retry whose changes had to reach the calculation definition actually executed. These cases connect diagnostics, interfaces, and recovery: feedback identifies the relevant object and relationship, the interface accepts a revised intention, and the next calculation supplies new facts.

Completion decisions involve this same connection. In IA1, an outstanding DAG requirement and a Supervisor endorsement both entered the agent's decision to finish. Specific subsequent feedback led it to change the delivery to partial. The global state preserves scientific obligations, while review comments help interpret routes and methodological limitations.

The system-level evaluation brings these two transitions together through the four-arm comparison. The smaller supplementary experiments follow this comparison and examine the stability of its conclusions under their respective conditions. Reading these results alongside the trajectories connects spatial preparation, evidence gathering, and delivery behavior to the central design question: how can research goals expressed in language remain attached to physical objects and scientific evidence?
